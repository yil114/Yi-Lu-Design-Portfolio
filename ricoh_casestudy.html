Overview

Ricoh's insurance platform was functional — but claim managers were drowning in it.

You might know Ricoh for printers. During this internship, I worked on their B2B software division. Insurance administrative costs in the US reach $158B annually. Ricoh had built a platform to help insurance companies manage claims — but the UX was actively slowing the people it was supposed to help.

This case study redesign touched three core flows: the Claim Work Queue, the Send Claim flow, and the Packet Detail view.

Images/Ricoh-Preview.pngHero shot of the redesigned Ricoh insurance platform
Main experience

Claim Work Queue

Shows all claims assigned to the manager — single documents or multi-document packets — along with status so they can track, manage, and prioritize work at a glance.

Send Claim

Lets claim managers send claims directly to end users. Enter their information, claim is delivered. Simple and minimal.

Packet Detail

View all documents in a packet — ID, received date, review status — then select the next document to validate.

Initial Research

The current system forced claim managers to fight their own tools every single day.

With the Product Manager, we mapped the pain points in the existing platform. What we found wasn't a single broken feature — it was a compounding set of small failures that made high-volume days genuinely unmanageable.

Pain Points

Key problems with the existing platform

  • —

    Users couldn't distinguish packet claims from individual claims at a glance.

  • —

    Color-coded statuses had no explanation — users were guessing throughout.

  • —

    No sense of progress through the validation queue. Tasks felt endless.

  • —

    High-volume days had no prioritization mechanism. Everything felt equally urgent.

  • —

    Multiple tab switches required to complete a single verification task.

Redesign

Three iterations. Each one a direct response to something Michael told us.

Nothing in this redesign was an aesthetic decision. Every change maps to a specific failure mode we observed in research.

Iteration 01

Show me what I care about

Claim managers need to see different claim types clearly — whether it's a packet or an individual claim. The original icon gave no indication of this, so every row looked identical regardless of complexity.

Before

Iteration 1 — BeforeSingle icon — only shows if a document is digitally delivered.

Single icon — packet vs. individual claim types are indistinguishable.

→ Claim managers couldn't prioritize by type.

After

Iteration 1 — AfterTwo distinct icons: one for packet claims, one for individual claims.

Two distinct icons: one for packet claims, one for individual claims.

→ Immediate visual differentiation at a glance.

Iteration 02

Why so many colors?

Distinguishing claim types solved one problem — but Michael was still confused by the color-coded status indicators. There was no legend, no label, no explanation. Users were guessing what each color meant.

Solution: simplified the color system to a clear, labeled status vocabulary. Removed ambiguous color-only encoding so every status was readable without prior knowledge.

Iteration 2 — Simplified color systemSimplified color system — labeled statuses replace ambiguous color-only encoding
Iteration 03

Show me how far I've come

With hundreds of forms to process, Michael had no sense of where he stood in the queue. The work felt endless because the interface gave him no signal that he was making progress. We added progress indicators showing completion percentage and remaining items in the validation queue.

Reflection

What I learned

Great design that can't ship isn't great design.

While working with the PM, he flagged that one of my design versions worked well — but would be too expensive to build. That conversation was formative. Business constraints aren't separate from the design brief. They are the design brief. Thinking about feasibility from the start is what separates work that looks good in a portfolio from work that actually reaches users.

No iteration is wasted.

Three iterations. Each one felt like starting over — and each one got us closer to right. The feedback from the PM and stakeholders after each round wasn't a setback; it was the process working as it should. The final solution exists because of the versions that didn't make it.
